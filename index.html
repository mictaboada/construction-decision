Construction safety decision

Do we send the crew out today?

You run a construction company. The forecast says extreme heat, and a crew is due on site. If they work, someone could get heat sick. If they stay home, you lose a day and money. Use the sliders to see what changes the odds, and where you would draw the line.

The issue

Heat illness runs from cramps and exhaustion to heat stroke, which can kill. In the US, about 40 workers a year die from heat on the job, and roughly a third of them are in construction (OSHA, BLS). Many more are sickened and never counted.

Two things decide how dangerous a hot day is: how hot it is (the hazard) and how ready the crew's bodies are (the vulnerability). Workers who are new to the heat, or back from a week off, have not adapted yet. OSHA reports that nearly half of heat deaths happen on a worker's first day.

This page assumes the workers are healthy adults aged 18 to 40. For them it also asks a physics question, livability: how hard can a young body work in this weather and still shed all the heat it makes? Past that point heat piles up inside the body.

The company has to weigh a lost day against that risk. Both sides of the choice can be costed, but only you can decide how much risk is acceptable.

What the sliders mean

  • Air temperature, humidity, sunshine. These set the hazard. They combine into the heat index, how hot it feels to the body.
  • How hard the work is. Effort in METs, where 1 is sitting still. Housework is about 3, dancing about 5, heavy lifting about 7.
  • Crew new to heat. The vulnerability. Share of workers in their first week of hot work, or back after a week or more away.
  • Crew size. How many people are exposed.
  • Start time, hours, breaks, protections. What you control: when people are outside, for how long, and with shade, water and checks.
  • Your limit. The most chance you will accept that at least one worker needs medical treatment for heat illness today.

What to notice

  • Heat multiplies risk. Every +6°F of heat index roughly doubles it. A few degrees matter a lot.
  • Vulnerability multiplies it again. Drag "crew new to heat" from 0% to 100% and the risk at every temperature goes up about ten times. The same hot day is mild for a veteran crew and dangerous for new hires.
  • They combine. In the first graph, the lines fan apart as it gets hotter. The extra damage from 5 more degrees is much larger for an inexperienced crew.
  • Livability falls fast with heat and humidity. In the map, watch the dot move toward the red zone as humidity rises. Raise "how hard the work is" and the dashed line moves left: hard work stops being livable at lower temperatures.
  • Timing is a lever. Try starting at 5 AM and ending before the afternoon peak in the day chart.
  • Stopping is not always the answer. Check the recommended plan at the bottom of the results.

What the model leaves out

Cold. Extreme cold is also dangerous, but I could not find sourced rates good enough to build a model, so this page covers heat only.

Other hazards. Lightning, smoke, ice, equipment limits and the details of each job.

Real risk is uncertain. The rates come from insurance claims and OSHA estimates, which undercount. Treat the outputs as orders of magnitude for exploring a decision, not predictions. Your safety officer and local rules, including state heat standards, come first.
